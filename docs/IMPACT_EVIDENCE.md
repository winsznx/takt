# Impact evidence

What this page claims, narrowly: a worker who thinks their hours were cut often
has fragments of the truth in different records (a schedule, the employer's
time record, pay stubs, messages), and a California wage claim asks them to
reconstruct and support what happened. It does not claim how many workers are
affected by any specific discrepancy, and it does not claim Takt changes agency
outcomes.

## 1. The claim process asks the worker to reconstruct hours and bring records

The Labor Commissioner's own filing guidance (pinned in
`legal/ca-dlse/instructions/how-to-file-wage-claim.html`, retrieved 2026-09-23
from https://www.dir.ca.gov/dlse/HowToFileWageClaim.htm):

> "Track all hours worked. Write down the time you begin and end work every
> day, when you take meal and rest breaks or heat recovery breaks, and the total
> hours you work."

> "Keep all your pay stubs"

> "By law, your employer is required to keep accurate records of actual time
> worked. … The Labor Commissioner requests these documents if you have them
> available, because they could help the Labor Commissioner's Office better
> understand your claim."

The official claim form (DLSE Form 1, REV. 07/2025) asks for the claimant's
typical start and end times for each day of the workweek and the amounts
earned and paid. For irregular hours, the forms page directs claimants to a
second worksheet (pinned in `legal/ca-dlse/instructions/dlse-forms.html`):

> "DLSE Form 55 for claimants who worked irregular hours and are claiming
> unpaid wages (for non-overtime or overtime hours worked) or meal and rest
> period violations"

Form 55 wants hours and pay per pay period. Filling it means adding up daily
times from other records, which is the work Takt automates and makes checkable.

## 2. Unpaid time before and after shifts is a documented pattern

National Employment Law Project, Center for Urban Economic Development (UIC),
and UCLA Institute for Research on Labor and Employment, *Broken Laws,
Unprotected Workers* (2009), a survey of 4,387 workers in low-wage industries
in Chicago, Los Angeles, and New York City
(https://www.nelp.org/app/uploads/2015/03/BrokenLawsReport2009.pdf).
The survey defines off-the-clock work as work "before or after a regularly
scheduled shift and for which no pay is provided." Its table of violations in
the week before the survey reports that 70.1% of workers at risk (those who
came in early or stayed late) were not paid for that off-the-clock work.

This is the scenario Takt's canonical case models: an earlier start supported
by a schedule and a manager's message that does not appear on the employer's
time record. The survey is from 2008 and covers three cities outside
California. It shows the pattern is real. It is not a current California
prevalence estimate.

## 3. Claims take a long time, so the worker's own evidence matters

California State Auditor, *Report 2023-104: The California Labor Commissioner's
Office* (May 29, 2024), https://www.auditor.ca.gov/reports/2023-104/:

- the Labor Commissioner's Office "took a median time of 854 days to issue a
  decision," against a 135-day statutory target;
- the backlog of unresolved wage claims was about 47,000 at the end of fiscal
  year 2022–23, up from about 22,000 in 2017–18;
- the audit names inadequate staffing as the main cause.

Takt does not reduce this backlog and does not claim to. The relevance is that
a worker may wait a long time, and records organized and fingerprinted when
the worker still has them are easier to rely on later.

## What this evidence does not show

- It does not show that any particular worker, employer, or industry has
  shaved time.
- It does not measure demand for Takt. Takt has not been tested with real
  workers. The fixtures in `fixtures/` are synthetic and are not user research.
- It does not show that a Takt packet will be accepted or will succeed.
